Geospatial Store Density and Regional Income Effects For Starbucks and Dunkin in the United States¶

Collaborators: Maeren Hay and Camden Yale

Project Motivations¶

Have you ever noticed that where you find a Starbucks seems different from where you find a Dunkin'? Our project uses store location data and census income figures to prove that this isn't random but instead calculated. We’re decoding the ultimate retail strategy: Dunkin' has achieved greater national reach, while Starbucks has perfected regional intensity. By running a statistical model, we can formally show how a city’s median income directly changes the probability of it being a Starbucks town versus a Dunkin' town.

This research is important because it uses two everyday coffee chains as a highly visible lens to reveal economic truths about America. By formally quantifying the Odds Ratios of these opposing strategies, we illustrate how major companies segment the nation based on wealth and population density, quantifying which brand has truly broken free of its founding region to achieve national dominance

Project Overview¶

Our primary goal is to provide a quantitative and comparative analysis of the market strategies for Starbucks and Dunkin' in the United States by focusing on their geospatial distribution and correlation with local economic conditions.

Objective: Logistic Regression Economic Correlation Model

We will develop a multi-stage Logistic Regression Model to statistically quantify how local demographics influence the presence and market domination of each chain.

  • Binary Logistic Regression

    • We will train two separate models (one for each chain) to determine the independent effect of median household income on the probability of a store's presence Starbucks_Present and Dunkin_Present.

  • Multinomial Logistic Regression

    • We will train a single model using the categorical dependent variable, Dominant_Brand, to analyze how median household income shifts the odds of a city falling into one of four market states (Neither, Starbucks Dominant, Dunkin Dominant, or Equal Competition).

Model Goals

  • Test the Income Hypothesis: Formally test the hypothesis that Dunkin' presence is negatively correlated with median household income, while Starbucks' presence is strongly driven by population density (POPESTIMATE2020) and is relatively neutral to income after controlling for city population size.
  • Quantify Market Odds: Use the Odds Ratios from the Multinomial model to quantify how regional status and economic conditions change the likelihood of a city being in a Dunkin' dominant market or an Equal Competition market, relative to the Starbucks-dominant market baseline.

Our Primary Research Questions

  1. Does the region of historical origin, the Northeast for Dunkin and the West for Starbucks, significantly affect the current geospatial presence of stores?

  2. Does a city's median household income correlate with the presence of Starbucks and Dunkins?

  3. Which chain is less reliant on its founding region for store existence, indicating greater national market reach and geographical spread?

  4. Which chain converts its founding region status into stronger competitive dominance and greater store density over its rival?

Dunkin Dataset

This first dataset is from Kaggle.com, by Collin Smith. It is a CSV containing U.S. Dunkin' Store Locations. Information was retrieved from Dunkin's location page at https://locations.dunkindonuts.com/en and was gathered with Python using the Requests and BeautifulSoup libraries. Lat/Long data was retrieved using the Geopy library. Collin states that the NaNs in the Latitude and Longitude columns indicate the geocoder couldn't locate them. Each observation in this table is a different U.S. Dunkin store with data in variables such as location, hours of operation, and store features. In addition to the locations and hours of operation variables, there is an int64 datatype row_id, which is our index, and multiple boolean type variables such as drive_thru, mobile_order, dunkin_card, kcup, curbside-pickup,and has_baskin_robbins. Then the loc_lat and loc_long variables are of type float64. All other variables like address, city, state, zip, phone, and hours are object and category datatypes. Overall, this table has 20 columns and 9248 rows. We plan to use this dataset to answer 3 of our main questions: Regional disparities in store locations between Dunkin and Starbucks in the Northeast/Elsewhere, North/South, and East/West. We will tidy this dataset to focus on the nominal and numerical data of locations and the quantity of stores. However, we hope that the boolean variables, such as curbside_pickup, can provide insight into marketing strategies when answering future questions. This dataset provides us with sufficient data to continue developing our model.

Model Methodology¶

Our chosen methodology is Logistic Regression (Binary and Multinomial). Logistic regression models the probability that an observation belongs to a particular class. It does this by estimating how each predictor changes the log-odds of an outcome, which we make into Odds Ratios to interpret the effects. We chose Logistic over Linear Regression because our dependent variables are binary (Present/Absent) or categorical (Dominance, Competition), not continuous numerical data which is required for Linear Regression. In predictive modeling using logistic regression, data is usually split into training and testing sets to evaluate how well a model generalizes unseen data. This project however is an explanatory model instead of a predictive one. Therefore we fit the model on the full dataset for an explanatory process. We will validate our model by using AUC-ROC and accuracy.

1. Data Merge and Cleaning

This initial stage ensures our city-level data is complete and structured for analysis.

  • Merging: Merge the Dunkin and Starbucks location data to get store counts per city. Merge the resulting store count data with the Median Household Income dataset to create income_analysis_df
  • Data Quality: Remove any duplicate rows and drop any rows containing missing values in the critical predictor variables to ensure clean input for the models.

2. Targets and Features

  • Create Target Variables

    • Binary Presence Indicator: Starbucks_Present and Dunkin_Present for the binary logistic regression model
    • Categorical Presence Indicator: Dominant_Brand for the multinomial logistic regression model

  • Create Regional Predictors

    • Starbucks_Founding_Region and Dunkin_Founding_Region

  • Log Transform Population

    • Apply a natural logarithm transformation POPESTIMATE2020 to the raw population data. This is necessary because city population data is heavily right-skewed, which is problematic for stable regression fitting.

3. Scaling

This final preparation step standardizes the continuous predictors to ensure they contribute equally to the model fitting process.

  • Scale Predictors
    • Scale the continous, independent variables (Median Income and POPESTIMATE2020 Log Transform)

4. Binary Logistic Regression

This is the first analytical stage, used to confirm the initial income correlation and regional hypotheses. (Research Question 1, 2, and 3)

  • Model Fitting: Fit two separate Binary Logistic Regression models: one for Starbucks_Present and one for Dunkin_Present.
  • Test Hypothesis: The Odds Ratio quantifies the directional change (positive/negative) in the odds of presence, dependent on income and independent of population and regional factors, allowing for a comparative analysis of national reach.

5. Multinomial Logistic Regression

This is the final analytical stage which provides the comprehensive market structure analysis required to answer our research questions.

  • Model Fitting: We fit a single Multinomial Logistic Regression model using the Dominant_Brand target, using Starbucks Dominant (1) as the reference baseline. This choice allows for direct competitive comparisons against the rival outcomes.

  • Test Hypothesis: We will utilize the separate Odds Ratios generated for each category to quantify:

    • The degree to which income and regional status change the odds of a city being in a Dunkin Dominant or Equal Competition market, relative to the Starbucks Dominant baseline.
    • The degree to which founding region status translates into a stronger competitive advantage by shifting the market odds toward dominance over the rival chain. 6. Model Validation and Reporting
      This final step ensures the reliability of the model and translates the quantitative output into final conclusions.

def process_sklearn_multinomial_results(model, X_cols):

    classes = model.classes_ # 0, 1, 2, 3

    # Map classes to readable statuses

        1: 'Starbucks Dom',
        2: 'Dunkin Dom',
        3: 'Equal Comp'
    }

    # Iterate through coefficients for each class (k-1 classes are outputted)
    for i, class_id in enumerate(classes[1:]): # classes[1:] skips class (0)
        comparison_name = f"{category_map[class_id]} vs. {category_map[classes[0]]}"

        cat_df = pd.DataFrame({
            'Predictor': X_cols, # Predictor names
            'Coefficient': model.coef_[i], # Coefficients for this class
            'Odds_Ratio': np.exp(model.coef_[i]), # Odds ratios are exponential coefficients
            'Comparison': comparison_name # The class we are using for comparison
        })
        multinomial_list.append(cat_df) # add this class's results table to the list
# Get all class tables into a single dataframe and reset index
    return pd.concat(multinomial_list).reset_index(drop=True)

While the founding region is the single strongest predictor for both chains, the magnitude of the effect defines a critical trade-off. Dunkin wins on reach, and Starbucks wins on intensity.

Dunkin has successfully executed a strategy that reaches broad geographic expansion and economic segmentation, making stores more accessible across less affluent cities. Conversly, Starbucks has perfected a strategy of high-density clustering and high intensity saturation. This creates deep concentrations of stores in dense metropolitan areas.
